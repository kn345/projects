01 · Executive Summary

What We Set Out to Solve

UW generates $1.74B in sponsored research annually yet no unified system exists to make it discoverable.

The University of Washington is one of the most research-intensive universities in the world, but researchers, industry partners, and the broader public face significant challenges navigating its vast research landscape. Faculty profiles are scattered across department sites, lab pages, and third-party academic databases often outdated and inconsistently formatted. Students seeking research mentors, companies seeking university partners, and journalists trying to find domain experts all encounter the same dead end: there's no good front door.

Our team commissioned through the Office of Research Information Services (ORIS) spent an academic year investigating this problem through intensive user research, competitive analysis, and iterative design. What emerged was not just a new website, but a rethinking of how a research university should represent itself to the world and to itself.

02 · Project Background

Understanding the Context

How a capstone brief about research discoverability evolved into something more complex and more interesting.

About ORIS

The Office of Research Information Services is the UW unit responsible for research data systems, faculty profiles, and the infrastructure that makes UW's research enterprise legible internally and externally. ORIS approached our team with a deceptively simple ask: help them understand what researchers and external stakeholders need from a research discovery platform.

What they knew was that existing tools weren't working. What they didn't know and what we set out to find was why, and what to build instead.

Evolving Business Focus

Early conversations with ORIS revealed a platform in transition. Existing efforts had largely focused on internal data systems tracking grants, managing compliance workflows, and reporting research metrics to administration. The external-facing layer was an afterthought: a directory of faculty names with limited profile information.

As UW's research enterprise grew, so did the need to tell its story outward to prospective students, industry collaborators, foundations, and policy makers. Our project sat at the inflection point of that shift.

The Two-Stream Problem

Through early research we identified two distinct but overlapping discoverability challenges:

External Visibility

The World Can't Find UW Research

Industry partners, policy makers, journalists, and prospective students have no reliable way to search, filter, or contextualize UW research. Academic databases like Google Scholar and PubMed are too technical for non-academic users. Department websites are siloed and inconsistently maintained.

Internal Visibility

Researchers Can't Find Each Other

Graduate students struggle to identify potential advisors aligned with their interests. Early-career faculty lack pathways to connect with complementary researchers. Collaboration happens through informal networks and word-of-mouth structural serendipity that systematically disadvantages newcomers.

Why One Platform

Early in our research we debated whether these were truly one problem or two. The answer: solving external discoverability well requires the same infrastructure needed to solve internal discoverability. Rich, structured, up-to-date researcher profiles serve both audiences. A robust search and filtering layer works for both a curious high schooler and a first-year PhD student. Building two separate systems would mean building the same foundation twice and maintaining it twice. One platform, designed thoughtfully for multiple audiences, was the right call.

Project Direction

We entered the project with a directive to design a research hub that surfaces UW research and researchers in a way that's accessible to non-academic users while remaining credible and useful to domain experts. What we built is Dub's Research Hub a platform that brings together researcher profiles, publications, labs, and projects into a unified, searchable, beautiful experience.

03 · Research Process

How We Investigated the Problem

A multi-phase, mixed-methods approach that kept us honest about what we knew and what we were assuming.

Snapshot of Our Overall Process

Phase 1: Scoping the Landscape

We started broad. Before talking to users, we needed to understand the competitive landscape and existing mental models around research discovery. Our secondary research phase covered academic publishing platforms, institutional repositories, professional networking sites, and science communication tools. We mapped 30+ platforms across four categories: discovery, networking, publishing, and learning.

Phase 2: Getting into the Weeds

Our survey gave us breadth patterns in how people currently find and engage with research. Our interviews gave us depth. We spoke with faculty across career stages, PhD students navigating advisor selection, industry professionals seeking academic partnerships, undergraduate researchers, and science communicators. Each conversation surfaced new assumptions to test and new tensions to sit with.

The Pivot

Midway through our research phase, we made a significant course correction. Our initial framing "how do external stakeholders discover UW research?" was too narrow. Interviews kept surfacing a deeper problem: the platform would only succeed if researchers themselves found value in maintaining their profiles. An external-facing directory of neglected profiles would be worse than useless. We reframed around a dual mandate: make research discoverable and give researchers intrinsic reasons to participate.

Affinity Mapping

After completing interviews we conducted affinity mapping sessions to cluster observations into themes. This synthesis process surfaced tensions and patterns that weren't obvious from individual transcripts particularly around the relationship between research visibility, career stage, and institutional belonging.

Key Research Findings

Six truths that shaped everything we built

01

AI-driven efficiency has accelerated publication volume

The number of papers published annually is growing faster than the human capacity to evaluate them. Researchers across career stages described feeling overwhelmed by the signal-to-noise ratio in their own fields let alone adjacent ones.

"I can't keep up with my own field. How would I know what's happening two doors down the hall?"
02

Who you know still determines who you trust and work with

Despite the proliferation of academic databases, collaboration decisions are still driven by personal referrals and conference relationships. Institutional affiliation and shared networks serve as proxies for trust when direct knowledge is absent.

"I've never cold-emailed a researcher I didn't already know through someone else. It feels presumptuous."
03

Early-to-mid career researchers face structural barriers

Graduate students and junior faculty are most dependent on the informal networks they have least access to. The Matthew effect advantages compounding for those already connected is highly visible in how research collaborations form.

"I don't know how to introduce myself to a professor I've never met. It's not like LinkedIn emailing out of the blue feels wrong."
04

Researcher profiles are scattered and outdated

The average UW faculty member maintains profiles on 4–7 different platforms, none of which are reliably current. Department websites are updated inconsistently; ORCID and Google Scholar require manual curation that researchers deprioritize.

"My department page still has my dissertation project listed as 'in progress.' I graduated four years ago."
05

Academia is structurally competitive

Several researchers expressed ambivalence about making their work "too visible" before publication. The competitive dynamics of academic publishing priority claims, scooping, grant competition create real disincentives for radical transparency about in-progress research.

"I want to collaborate, but I also don't want to show my hand before I publish."
06

External stakeholders find academic databases intimidating

Industry professionals, policy makers, and science journalists consistently described existing academic databases as inaccessible. Jargon-heavy abstracts, inconsistent metadata, and interface design optimized for experts create a literacy barrier for non-academic audiences.

"I know the research is out there. I just can't make sense of what I find when I go looking."

User Personas

From our research we synthesized four primary personas that guided design decisions throughout the project: the graduate student seeking mentorship, the early-career faculty building reputation, the industry partner seeking collaboration, and the curious non-expert.

04 · Design Principles

The Values Behind Every Decision

Six principles distilled from research, debated by the team, and tested against every design choice we made.

Principle 01

Balance automation with researcher autonomy

AI can surface insights and fill gaps, but researchers must remain in control of how they're represented. Automated profile population is helpful automated misrepresentation is dangerous. Every AI-generated element should be transparent and editable.

Principle 02

Design for expert-agnostic collaboration

The platform must serve a curious high schooler and a tenured professor equally well. This doesn't mean dumbing down academic content it means layering complexity thoughtfully, so each user finds the depth they need without hitting a wall.

Principle 03

Align with existing research workflows

Researchers won't adopt a new tool that creates more work. The platform must integrate with how research is already documented ORCID, institutional repositories, grant systems rather than requiring manual re-entry of information that exists elsewhere.

Principle 04

Preserve the joy of research

Discovery should feel delightful. Whether browsing unexpected research connections or reading a researcher's plain-language description of their work, the platform should evoke intellectual curiosity, not administrative obligation.

Principle 05

Surface holistic trust signals

Citation counts alone are insufficient and often misleading proxies for research quality. The platform should surface richer trust signals: mentorship history, collaboration networks, industry partnerships, and community engagement especially for early-career researchers.

Principle 06

Amplify research visibility

Great research that no one can find is incomplete research. The platform has a responsibility to surface work from historically underrepresented researchers and institutions, and to measure success not just in traffic but in equitable discovery.

Sketching & Ideation

We started with rapid paper sketching no fidelity constraints, no UI conventions, just a shared prompt: "what does research discovery feel like when it works?" Each team member sketched independently before sharing, giving us four diverse starting points to synthesize from. Concepts ranged from timeline-based research visualization to network-graph exploration to editorial magazine-style browsing.

Systems Design

Before jumping into screens, we mapped the system. Who are all the actors? What data flows where? What are the dependencies between researcher profiles, publications, projects, and labs? This systems-level thinking surfaced important design constraints particularly around how data would be sourced and kept current and shaped our information architecture decisions.

User Journey Mapping

We created journey maps for each primary persona, tracing their current-state experience of research discovery and a future-state vision using Dub's Research Hub. The journey maps revealed key pain-point moments the "dead end" when a department website is clearly out of date, the confusion when a publication list doesn't link to full text and "moments of delight" we could amplify, like the serendipitous discovery of adjacent research.

Information Architecture

Wireframing

With IA established, we moved into low-fidelity wireframes for key flows:

Onboarding

Progressive profile setup that imports from ORCID and institutional systems, reducing friction for new researchers joining the platform.

Discovery

Search and browse interfaces optimized for both keyword-intent users and exploratory browsers, with faceted filtering and contextual recommendations.

Profile

Researcher profile pages that balance professional credibility signals with accessible plain-language summaries and visible collaboration hooks.

Learning

The Learn section wireframes explored editorial, magazine-style layouts that present research narratively rather than bibliographically.

Design Synthesis

Between wireframing and concept testing, we ran a synthesis session to identify which design directions had the strongest signal and which were competing with each other. This process cut our concept space from dozens of ideas to a focused set of testable concepts.

Concept Testing Results

We ran 14 concept tests with participants spanning our four primary personas. Concepts ranged from specific UI patterns (the "Network Tree" visualization for collaboration graphs) to broader navigation paradigms (timeline-based browsing of a researcher's work).

What Worked

  • Shortlist / Save feature for building research reading lists
  • Learn section editorial cards for non-expert entry
  • Network Tree visual collaboration graph on profiles
  • Rich researcher profiles with explicit contact hooks
  • Topic-based browsing with visual hierarchy

What Didn't Land

  • Timeline view of researcher's career arc (felt surveillance-y)
  • Research Reels short-form video summaries felt out of place
  • Public "looking for collaborators" status too vulnerable for early career
  • Gamified profile completion progress bars

06 · Ethical Assessment

Designing Responsibly at Scale

A platform that aggregates and surfaces information about people carries inherent ethical weight. We took that seriously.

Value-Sensitive Design Framework

We applied the Value-Sensitive Design (VSD) framework throughout our design process not as a checklist, but as a genuine inquiry into whose values the platform encodes, who benefits, and who might be harmed. VSD asks us to consider direct stakeholders, indirect stakeholders, and non-users who may nonetheless be affected by the system's existence.

Mild vs. Wild: Risk Spectrum Mapping

We mapped potential ethical risks along a "mild to wild" spectrum from predictable, manageable concerns to speculative but high-stakes scenarios. This exercise prevented us from fixating only on obvious risks (privacy) while ignoring subtler ones (prestige amplification, AI misrepresentation).

Stakeholder Mapping

We identified four primary stakeholder groups whose interests must be held in tension:

UW researchers (faculty, postdocs, graduate students)
Industry and non-profit partners seeking collaboration
Students and prospective students
General public, journalists, and policy makers

Risks & Mitigations

The platform aggregates publicly available information about researchers from multiple sources. Even when each individual piece of data is public, aggregation creates a comprehensive profile that researchers may not have consented to in spirit, even if they've consented technically by publishing.

Mitigation Researchers receive profiles they can review, edit, suppress, or delete. Automated imports are clearly labeled as "auto-populated please review." Researchers can opt out of aggregation from external sources while maintaining a manual profile.

Any recommendation algorithm that surfaces "popular" or "highly cited" research risks amplifying the already-advantaged. Researchers from prestigious labs, well-funded fields, and dominant demographic groups are already more visible a naïve recommendation system would make this worse.

Mitigation We explicitly avoid citation-count-based ranking as a primary signal. Curation surfaces emerging researchers and underrepresented fields. Algorithmic decisions are audited quarterly for demographic bias, with results reported to ORIS leadership.

AI-generated profile summaries and research plain-language descriptions risk putting words in researchers' mouths. A hallucinated claim about a researcher's specialization or positions could damage professional reputation, create legal liability for UW, or spread misinformation.

Mitigation All AI-generated content is labeled as "AI-generated summary not reviewed by researcher" until the researcher approves it. Researchers receive email prompts to review auto-generated content. A feedback mechanism flags suspect AI outputs for human review.

A platform designed to facilitate industry-academia collaboration could, if monetized or pressured, begin to subtly favor industry-friendly research topics or researchers with industry ties. This would compromise the platform's integrity as a neutral representation of UW's research.

Mitigation The platform is operated by ORIS as a public-interest service, with no advertising or industry sponsorship. Industry partnership links are clearly labeled and researcher-controlled. Governance includes faculty representation to resist undue commercial influence.

Phased Rollout

We recommended a phased rollout to allow trust to be built incrementally and course-corrections to be made before full public launch:

Phase 1 Internal Beta

UW community only

Limited to UW NetID login. Faculty and grad students can claim and customize profiles. No public indexing. Core search and browse functionality validated internally before external exposure.

Phase 2 Public Preview

Read-only public access

Profiles visible to the public without login. Researchers have had 6 months to review and update their profiles. Contact pathways enabled. AI content has been reviewed and approved by researchers.

Phase 3 Full Launch

Full platform with all features

Search, browse, Learn section, and collaboration pathways fully operational. Ongoing algorithmic auditing in place. Feedback mechanisms active. ORIS governance framework operational.

Long-Term Vision

Dub's Research Hub is not just a directory it's an argument about what a public research university owes its community and the world. UW's research is publicly funded, in large part. Making it discoverable, legible, and connected is part of UW's responsibility to the taxpayers and students who make it possible. If this platform succeeds, it becomes a model for how research universities everywhere can make their intellectual output genuinely public.

07 · Reflection

What We Learned

Looking back honestly on a year of design work what surprised us, what challenged us, and what we'd do differently.

On the pivot: Reframing the brief mid-project

The decision to expand our frame beyond "external discoverability" to include internal researcher networking was uncomfortable in the moment it felt like scope creep at exactly the wrong time in the project. In retrospect, it was the most important decision we made. A platform built only for external audiences would have produced a ghost town: beautiful profiles that no one maintained because researchers saw nothing in it for them. By ensuring the platform delivers value to researchers first, we gave the external audience something real to discover.

The pivot also forced us to confront a tension that ran through the rest of the project: what happens when researcher autonomy conflicts with institutional visibility goals? We don't think we fully resolved that tension but naming it clearly shaped every design decision that followed.

On research: What we found vs. what we expected

We went into the project expecting researchers to want a better academic database a smarter Google Scholar. What we found was more human and more political than that. Researchers don't primarily have an information problem; they have a trust and connection problem. The tools exist to find papers. What doesn't exist is a way to build relationships across the disciplinary walls that fragment even a single university.

"I know there's someone in Engineering working on something directly relevant to my lab. I just don't know who, and I have no way to find out without asking my department chair, who doesn't know either."

This quote from one of our faculty interviews reframed the problem for us. The question wasn't "how do we index research?" it was "how do we make serendipitous, valuable connections feel possible and safe?"

On design: The tension we never fully resolved

We spent months trying to design a profile page that would simultaneously serve a curious high schooler, a prospective PhD student, a venture capitalist, and a peer academic reviewer. These audiences want radically different things from the same page. We tried layered disclosure, we tried audience selectors, we tried showing the most-accessible version by default with depth behind a "for experts" toggle.

None of these felt completely satisfying. What we shipped in the prototype is our best attempt at a layered design that doesn't overwhelm a newcomer or bore an expert but we'd be dishonest if we claimed the problem was solved rather than managed. A future version of this platform would benefit from actual A/B testing with both audience types simultaneously.

What would you have changed?

More time with non-academic external stakeholders. Our interview sample skewed heavily toward the UW community faculty, grad students, undergrads with only a handful of industry professionals and zero journalists or policy makers interviewed in depth. Given that external accessibility was one of our two core mandates, this was a real gap. We made up for it partially with our survey and secondary research, but first-person interviews with journalists trying to find academic sources, or program officers evaluating grant applications, would have surfaced needs we're certain we missed.

We'd also have loved more time to test the Learn section. It was the most novel element of the platform editorially curated research communication for a non-specialist audience and we had the least validation data on it. The concept tests suggested it was compelling, but we didn't have time to test the actual content production workflow with researchers asked to write for it.

What were the unexpected challenges?

The hardest challenge wasn't design it was data. Every feature we designed assumed that rich, structured, accurate researcher profile data would exist. In reality, UW's research data infrastructure is fragmented across dozens of systems that don't talk to each other well. Our designs are contingent on data work that ORIS hasn't yet completed. We tried to design with this constraint in mind building in manual fallbacks, progressive disclosure, and graceful degradation but there's no escaping the fact that the platform's quality is a ceiling set by data quality.

The other unexpected challenge was the emotional valence of research visibility. We didn't anticipate how vulnerable researchers would feel about having their work made more visible. Imposter syndrome, fear of being publicly wrong, discomfort with self-promotion these are real forces that shape whether researchers will engage with a platform like this. Design can help by reducing friction, but it can't eliminate the underlying cultural dynamics of academia.

Universal Design Principles

Data Privacy

Total control over data sensitivity, ensuring researcher anonymity and data integrity are never compromised. Researchers decide what is visible, to whom, and when.

Context-Aware

Beyond the database: an adaptive system that understands user intent to eliminate information overload. The platform surfaces what is relevant to each user rather than presenting everything at once.

Impact-First

A high-visibility tool built to showcase University of Washington's research impact and facilitate collaboration. The platform leads with the real-world significance of research, not just its metadata.
